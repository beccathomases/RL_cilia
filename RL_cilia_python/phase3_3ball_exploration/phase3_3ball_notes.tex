\section{Goal of this phase}

The goal of this phase is to move beyond the two-ball benchmark and begin exploring a richer cilium model with three segments / three balls / three relative angles. The main motivation is that the two-ball benchmark, while very useful, seemed to support only a fairly narrow family of strong strokes. Adding one more joint broadens the accessible stroke space and gives a better test of which conclusions from the benchmark survive in a more expressive geometry.

I also want to begin in a regime where value iteration and tabular methods are still feasible, so that I can compare exact, tabular, and neural methods on the same benchmark.

\section{3-ball environment definition}

The current three-ball environment is intended as the simplest natural extension of the two-ball benchmark.

\subsection*{Geometry and state representation}

The cilium is modeled as three equal-length segments with fixed total length:
\[
L_1=L_2=L_3=\frac13.
\]

As in the two-ball case, the environment stores \emph{relative} angles,
\[
\phi = (\phi_1,\phi_2,\phi_3),
\]
where $\phi_1$ is the base angle and the physical segment angles are reconstructed cumulatively:
\[
\psi_1=\phi_1,\qquad
\psi_2=\phi_1+\phi_2,\qquad
\psi_3=\phi_1+\phi_2+\phi_3.
\]

This preserves the same serial-link convention used in the two-ball code and keeps the interpretation of the coordinates consistent across phases.

\subsection*{Angle ranges and discretization}

The current working discretization is
\[
n_{\mathrm{bins}} = [11,21,21],
\]
with angle ranges
\[
\phi_1 \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{4},\frac{\pi}{4}\right],\qquad
\phi_2,\phi_3 \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right].
\]

This keeps the base-angle range comparable to the two-ball benchmark while allowing the internal joints enough freedom to produce richer motions.

\subsection*{Actions}

The action space is the natural extension of the two-ball setting: each relative angle can increment by $-1$, $0$, or $1$ bin per step, excluding the all-zero action. Thus the total number of actions is
\[
3^3-1=26.
\]

\subsection*{Boundary handling and reward}

The reward is still the same one-step flux-like quantity derived from the Stokes/image-system solve, together with the same general boundary-handling logic (`clip\_penalty' or `stay\_penalty').

\section{What turned out to matter immediately}

\subsection*{Numerical safeguards}

In the first three-ball experiments, the raw reward table contained very large outliers coming from near-singular hydrodynamic solves. Adding simple numerical safeguards---for example rejecting near-contact configurations and very large finite force/flux values---was necessary before value iteration became interpretable.

\subsection*{Physical wall admissibility}

Even after the numerical blow-up was controlled, value iteration initially found a short local cycle that dipped below the wall. This made it clear that angle-box constraints alone are not enough once the geometry is richer: the environment also needs a physical admissibility condition.

The fix used here was to require that all ball centers remain at least one radius above the wall:
\[
z_i > a
\]
for each ball center, where $a$ is the ball radius.

After adding this condition, the short degenerate cycle disappeared and value iteration produced a longer coordinated recurrent motion.

\section{Value iteration on the three-ball benchmark}

With the wall-clearance condition and the numerical safeguards in place, value iteration on the refined three-ball benchmark
\[
[11,21,21]
\]
produced a cycle of length $23$ with average reward
\[
0.797059.
\]

This is the highest average reward recorded so far on the refined three-ball benchmark among the methods tested here. The corresponding cycle serves as the current benchmark reference.

\section{Tabular Q-learning on the refined three-ball benchmark}

I next tested tabular Q-learning on the same refined three-ball environment with discretization
\[
[11,21,21].
\]

A practical point here was that the episode horizon matters. Shorter episode lengths often led to shorter or less satisfactory learned cycles, while longer runs with sufficiently long episodes produced a more coherent picture.

Across five seeds, the best tabular-Q cycles had lengths between $21$ and $23$, and average rewards between about $0.70$ and $0.76$. Thus the tabular-Q runs were close to the value-iteration benchmark in cycle length, but did not exceed the VI average reward of $0.797059$.

\section{DQN on the refined three-ball benchmark}

I next tested DQN on the same refined physically admissible three-ball benchmark used for value iteration and tabular Q-learning.

Across the first three seeds, the best detected cycle lengths were
\[
47,\qquad 24,\qquad 23,
\]
with corresponding average rewards
\[
0.777902,\qquad 0.781296,\qquad 0.797059.
\]

Thus, two of the three DQN runs landed very close to the VI/tabular cycle-length scale, while the remaining run produced a longer cycle of length $47$. The best DQN average reward matched the VI benchmark value to the recorded precision, but did not exceed it.

The length-$47$ DQN cycle appears to lie in the same broad stroke family as the shorter DQN, VI, and tabular-Q cycles, but in a longer and less sharply resolved realization.

\section{PPO on the refined three-ball benchmark}

I also tested PPO on the same refined physically admissible three-ball benchmark used for value iteration, tabular Q-learning, and DQN.

In the first small PPO sweep, all recorded best cycles had length $22$. Their average rewards were below the VI benchmark value $0.797059$. Thus, on the runs recorded so far, PPO recovered the same broad stroke family in cycle length and geometry, but did not match or exceed the VI average reward.
